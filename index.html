Prosecutor • Criminologist • Public Servant

                            

                                
Individual liberty does not survive in a vacuum; it requires architecture. The freest societies on Earth, from the constitutional traditions of the Enlightenment to the high-trust social democracies of Norway and Denmark, rest on a single bargain: citizens grant the state a monopoly on coercion and pool their wealth for the common welfare only when they can trust that public power will be exercised with candor, proportionality, and restraint. Where the law is arbitrary, liberty withers. Where the public peace collapses, the vulnerable pay the tax first.

                                
The prosecutor sits at the fulcrum of that bargain. Alone among officers of the court, the prosecutor holds the sovereign discretion to decide when a private wrong becomes a public indictment and what measure of freedom the state will ask a court to take. That authority is not an adversarial weapon designed to maximize convictions. It is a fiduciary duty to the social contract. A prosecution worthy of a free commonwealth must be fierce enough to defend the public commons against violence, fraud, and predation, yet disciplined enough never to mistake the machinery of punishment for justice itself.

                                
Sound prosecution therefore treats the courtroom not as a theater of retribution, but as an instrument of civic stewardship. Drawing on felony and misdemeanor prosecution, indigent defense, judicial clerkship, and empirical criminology, the work proceeds from a simple premise: a strong welfare state and a rigorous rule of law are partners, not rivals. When the state charges only what the admissible record proves beyond a reasonable doubt, diverts what social intervention can mend, and prosecutes genuine harm without fear or favor, it earns the legitimacy on which every human freedom depends.

                            

                        


                        

                            
AI Post-Training Specialist • Legal Futurist • Systems Architect

                            

                                
Civilization is in the middle of migrating its institutional memory and analytical labor from human bureaucracies to probabilistic machines. Every month, courts, law firms, and corporate enterprises delegate a larger share of their reasoning to frontier language models capable of traversing a fifty-file data room in seconds and fabricating a dispositive fact in the same breath. The future is not an external event waiting to be forecast. It is an institutional architecture being compiled right now, and whether it enlarges human liberty or erodes it depends entirely on how its foundations are engineered.

                                
In a high-trust liberal society, power is legitimate only when it is auditable, bounded by rules, and answerable to reason. That principle applies with equal force to a parliament, a prosecutor, and a transformer model. Leaving the alignment of legal and enterprise AI to raw scale and vendor optimism invites a quiet feudalism of black-box decisions. Constructing a future worth inhabiting requires the harder craft of epistemic engineering: designing deterministic evaluation rubrics, auditing Chain-of-Thought reasoning traces across complex document repositories, and enforcing closed-universe evidentiary grounding so that machine intelligence remains a disciplined servant of human judgment.

                                
The work presented here operates at the junction of frontier AI post-training, programmatic automation, and institutional governance. By combining fifteen years of cross-border corporate structuring and adversarial trial practice with hands-on RLHF/SFT architecture, Python docket automation, and municipal AI policy design, the objective is straightforward: to build intelligent systems that accelerate human capability without ever asking society to take a black box on faith.

The central problem of political philosophy is how strangers of wildly different origins and convictions can share a polity without surrendering either their autonomy or their security. Classical liberalism answered half of that question by erecting constitutional walls around the individual: due process, the presumption of innocence, and strict limits on the sovereign. Modern social democracy answered the other half by recognizing that negative liberty is hollow without material dignity. A citizen is not meaningfully free merely because the state leaves them alone in the cold; real freedom requires public institutions strong enough to educate, insure, rehabilitate, and protect.

                                    
Criminal law is the perimeter defense of that social order, and its moral authority depends entirely on its precision. In a high-trust society, the criminal docket is not a trash bin for unaddressed social policy. Poverty, addiction, and mental illness belong primarily to the welfare state; violence, exploitation, and calculated harm belong to the prosecutor. Confusing the two corrupts both: it turns courts into engines of attrition and starves serious cases of the forensic attention they demand.

                                    
Empirical criminology confirms what liberal ethics has long argued: human behavior responds to the certainty and fairness of the law far more than to performative cruelty. Having litigated across the aisle as a public defender, drafted dispositive orders from behind the judicial bench, structured cross-border corporate governance, and prosecuted criminal dockets in Alaska and Arizona, the lesson across every seat in the courtroom is identical. Procedural rigor is not a technicality that shelters the guilty; it is the quality-control mechanism of the truth.

                                    
When the state speaks in a courtroom, it speaks with the borrowed voice of every citizen. It must therefore be the most honest party in the room: disclosing weaknesses in its own file before being asked, auditing police narratives against forensic reality, and calibrating every sentencing recommendation to verifiable risk rather than political theater. Law is the skeleton of the commonwealth. Keeping it straight is the work.

                                


                                

                                    
Every transformative technology triggers the same political question: will the new machinery concentrate power in the hands of a few, or will it be domesticated by law and design to raise the floor for everyone? The great achievement of the modern welfare state, visible in the Nordic model and the best of the liberal constitutional tradition, was proving that technological and economic abundance can be paired with universal dignity when institutions are designed with foresight. Artificial intelligence is the next test of that thesis.

                                    
Current frontier models do not fail on complex legal and corporate workflows because they lack fluency; they fail because fluency is easily mistaken for truth. In high-stakes domains such as M&A due diligence, multi-site portfolio acquisitions, and statutory compliance, a model that adopts an unverified premise, misses a negative constraint, or hallucinates across conflicting disclosure schedules is not merely making a software bug. It is introducing silent systemic risk into the legal and financial bloodstream.

                                    
Solving that problem requires treating AI post-training and evaluation as an applied science of verification. Through work on frontier agentic benchmarks (including Mercor's Project Julius), the focus lies in constructing multi-hop evaluation environments across fifty-plus-file virtual data rooms, authoring gold-standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) reference responses, and red-teaming model Chain-of-Thought trajectories. When a model's reasoning is graded against atomic, deterministic rubrics that penalize unsupported extrapolation, probabilistic generation is forced to respect evidentiary boundaries.

                                    
Downstream from model training lies deployment architecture and governance. Whether engineering Python data-extraction scripts that triaged a nine-hundred-case judicial docket on the Superior Court of the Virgin Islands, drafting municipal generative AI usage and local-inference policies in Juneau, or structuring copyright-compliant training-data daemons and UK GDPR data-processing frameworks, the governing philosophy remains constant. We construct the future by writing the rules into the code before the code becomes the rule.

In a liberal democracy, the prosecutor is designated a minister of justice precisely because the state is not an ordinary litigant. A private party enters court to win a dispute; the sovereign enters court to vindicate a public norm without breaking a higher one. That mandate divides into three functions. As the gatekeeper, the prosecutor filters raw allegations through the sieve of admissible proof and constitutional fidelity before a citizen's name is ever placed on an indictment. As the advocate, the prosecutor presents the surviving truth to the court with forensic clarity and unshakeable preparation. And as the steward, the prosecutor aligns every disposition with the long-term health of the community. Remove any one of the three, and law degenerates into mere force.

                        

                        

                            
Building intelligence fit for legal and institutional infrastructure requires three interlocking disciplines. First comes epistemic architecture: designing the evaluation environments, multi-hop tasks, and atomic rubrics that teach a model the difference between plausible prose and verified fact. Second comes adversarial auditing: dissecting Chain-of-Thought traces and tool-use trajectories to catch subtle reasoning failures before they cascade through an enterprise. Third comes institutional governance: translating copyright law, privilege rules, and data-privacy mandates into programmatic guardrails. Without architecture, models drift; without auditing, they hallucinate; without governance, they cannot be lawfully deployed.
